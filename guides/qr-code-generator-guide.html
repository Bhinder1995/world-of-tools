Introduction: The Resurgence of the QR Code

Invented in 1994 for tracking automotive parts, the Quick Response (QR) code spent years as a niche technology before exploding into the mainstream. Today, they are ubiquitous—found on everything from cereal boxes to high-end business cards and government IDs. They allow for an instant transfer of data from physical objects to mobile devices, eliminating the need to type long URLs or complex passwords. How a QR Code Works (The Basics)

Unlike a standard barcode which stores data only in one dimension (horizontally), a QR code stores data in two dimensions (up-down and left-right). This "matrix" structure allows it to store up to 7,000 numeric characters—hundreds of times more than a standard retail barcode.

  • Position Markers: The three large squares in the corners help the scanner determine the orientation and size of the code.
  • Data Modules: The remaining dots and squares represent the actual encoded information (bits).
  • Error Correction: QR codes are designed to remain functional even if they are partially damaged or dirty. Some codes can survive up to 30% data loss and still scan perfectly!

🎨 Design Tip: Contrast is Key

While you can change the color of a QR code, always ensure there is high contrast between the code (foreground) and the background. A light grey code on a white background will often fail to scan. For maximum reliability, stick to dark foreground colors and light backgrounds.
